\# Signal Post 3 — Real Value or FOMO?



\## AI scouting: finding undervalued footballers or selling the dream?



My beat is how football clubs use data to find players whose performance is better than their reputation or transfer value suggests. One AI vendor that fits this beat particularly well is Oraca, which markets itself as an AI football scouting platform for recruitment teams.



Oraca makes a strong promise: it says it can turn nearly 20,000 players into the small number worth scouts' attention. It also says its AI is trained on twenty years of European football and 300 million data points. Most importantly for my beat, Oraca claims its system can identify players whose performance is ahead of their current price and profile.



Some of these statements are relatively concrete. Oraca states that it covers more than 60 leagues across more than 20 countries, monitors nearly 20,000 players and refreshes its data daily. These are claims that could potentially be checked. The company also presents a specific backtest: between 2018 and 2023, it says its system flagged 327 players, with recommended players returning an average of 4.8 times their transfer fee and 12% returning more than 10 times their fee.



But this is where the marketing starts to become harder to evaluate.



“Turns nearly 20,000 players into the handful worth your scouts' time” is not a measurable claim without knowing how “worth your scouts' time” is defined. The same applies to the promise that the system can identify players who are “about to be worth far more than they cost today.” That is a prediction about the future, not a simple fact.



The backtest is more interesting because it gives actual numbers. However, the website does not provide enough information to reproduce the result. I would want to know the model's precision and false-positive rate, what counted as a successful recommendation, how transfer fees were handled, what the baseline comparison was, and whether the model was tested out-of-sample. A historical backtest can look impressive while still being affected by selection bias or hindsight.



The FOMO framework gives me a similar conclusion. The specific business problem is real: clubs have limited scouting resources and cannot manually investigate every player in every league. Doing nothing could mean missing undervalued players. But the cost of that problem is not quantified by Oraca. Its pricing and total cost are also not obvious from the public pitch, so a club would need to consider the subscription, data, integration and staff time.



There is also a simpler alternative for some scouting problems. In my Week 2 experiment, a spreadsheet could reliably filter players using fixed rules such as age, minutes, goals and assists and market value. AI becomes more interesting when the problem involves ranking thousands of players, combining many signals or processing unstructured scouting information.



My verdict is therefore \*\*pilot, not buy\*\*. There is real AI potential here, and the problem is genuinely suited to large-scale data analysis. But Oraca's strongest claims still need independent verification. A club should run a controlled pilot with pre-agreed success metrics before treating the marketing promise as proven value.



\*\*Source:\*\* Oraca, “AI football scouting for recruitment teams”, accessed October 2026.
