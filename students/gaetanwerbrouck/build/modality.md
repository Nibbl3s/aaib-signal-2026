\# Week 2 - Borderline Modality Cases



\## Case 1 - AI-generated player scouting shortlist



\*\*Classification:\*\* AI-suitable with verification



A football club could use AI to create an initial shortlist of players who match a set of scouting criteria. The input could include structured statistics such as age, minutes played, goals and assists per 90, position and estimated market value. The output would be a shortlist of players for a human scout to investigate further.



This is relatively suitable for AI because the task can be bounded by explicit criteria and the output can be checked against the underlying data. The main risk is that the AI could use outdated information, misunderstand a condition, or invent supporting information about a player. The shortlist should therefore be treated as a first-pass screening tool rather than a final scouting decision.



The modality is relatively low-risk when the AI is only narrowing a large pool of players and a scout verifies the results before acting on them.



\---



\## Case 2 - AI estimating a player's transfer value



\*\*Classification:\*\* AI-risky



Estimating a player's transfer value is more difficult because the answer depends on many changing and uncertain factors. Performance statistics matter, but so do age, contract length, club, league, demand from other clubs, injuries, reputation and the timing of the transfer market.



An AI model could produce a plausible estimate, but there is no single correct answer that can always be verified in advance. Market values also change over time, so an answer that was reasonable several months ago could become outdated.



AI could therefore be useful as one input when comparing players or generating a range of possibilities, but it should not be treated as an objective transfer price or used as the only basis for a major financial decision.



\---



\## Case 3 - AI predicting injury risk



\*\*Classification:\*\* AI-risky



AI could analyse information such as playing minutes, workload, previous injuries and match schedules to estimate injury risk. However, injury prediction contains significant uncertainty and depends on information that may be incomplete or difficult to measure.



A wrong prediction could also have important consequences for recruitment and player management. Unlike my player-screening experiment, there is no simple five-rule test that can reliably determine whether the prediction is correct.



AI could support medical or performance staff by identifying patterns that deserve further investigation, but the output would need to be interpreted by qualified professionals and should not be treated as a definitive prediction.



\---



\## Overall observation



The important difference between these cases is not simply whether AI is technically capable of producing an answer. It is whether the output is appropriate for the task and how much uncertainty can be tolerated.



An AI-generated shortlist can be useful when the criteria are explicit and the results can be verified. Transfer-value estimation and injury-risk prediction are more uncertain because the underlying factors change and the correct answer is harder to establish.



This means that AI usefulness depends on the \*\*task, the input data, the required output and the consequences of being wrong\*\*, not just on whether an AI system can generate an answer.
