## Checkpoint 1 — end of Week 4

| # | Skill | Rating | Evidence |
|---|-------|--------|----------|
| 1 | Cost literacy | 🟢 | I understood why the cheapest AI model is not always the best choice economically. For example, using AI in German can require between 24.7% and 96.3% more tokens than in English, depending on the input/output. Furthermore, token costs are not the only factor to consider. Error correction and the resulting human workload can outweigh the savings from choosing a cheaper model.|
| 2 | Capability skepticism | 🟡 | I implemented my Feedback-to-Task Assistant for project managers in creative agencies. The tests showed me that AI can produce good and reliable results, but it can also fail when dealing with ambiguous feedback where the required action is unclear (e.g., test case T10). I would still like to investigate how the tool performs with different types of feedback. |
| 3 | Vendor claim detection | 🟡 | I learned that AI should not automatically be applied to every process. Instead, we should first ask ourselves whether AI is actually necessary. I also realized that vendor claims should not always be taken at face value. In the NexusAI analysis, I evaluated 16 claims and classified them as Fact, Aspiration or Avoidance. However, I still need more practice distinguishing between these categories confidently. |
| 4 | Value quantification | 🟡 | Analyzing the cost structure of my own tool was particularly interesting and helped me reinforce my knowledge from Week 1. My business case showed a potential annual net benefit of €5,325 from using AI to process client feedback. The calculation remained positive even when I reduced the estimated time savings by 50%. However, my assumptions were mainly based on my own experience working as a project manager in a creative agency rather than independently validated data, which is still a weakness of my analysis. |

## Checkpoint 2 — end of Week 8

| 5 | Market mechanism thinking | 🟢 | feel very confident here because I used the knowledge from class and adapted it to the company where I work. I had a call with the AI responsible and made a deeper GEO analysis tailored to Mutabor, which I presented to my colleague. We came to the conclusion that my research was mostly equal to the research my colleague had already done. However, it was valuable because it gave more evidence to the existing research, which will be adapted for our website and our PR work in the following months. One criterion, for example, is that the source code of our website isn't AI-friendly, which means the AI can't get all important information from it. I also found that even though we have PR in reliable websites, niche websites also count for AI. That's not so important for SEO, but for AI it is. |
| 6 | Cost projection | 🟢 | As we discussed cost literacy and cost projection a lot during the last week, this is a topic where I feel confident. I tested it with my own built AI and found a discrepancy between AI costs and error costs: token costs are about 35 cents, while correction costs in my particular build are €191. That led me to the conclusion that it is valuable for PMs to have a Feedback-to-Task assistant, because AI costs are much cheaper than labour costs, but only if the labour time saved doesn't have to be used to correct the errors. Correction errors cost a lot more than tokens.|
| 7 | Lock-in detection | 🟢 | I understood that even if you decide on a tool, you can't safely rely on it, and you always need to build ways out of the tool, for example when costs become extraordinarily high or the tool stops working for the company. What I found for the company I am looking at is that Langdock scores 5.5/15, which is moderate risk. That's good, but not perfect. The main reason is that Langdock combines different tools (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini), so a change from Langdock to a single vendor would be manageable. Another advantage is that it is easy to export and save the data from Langdock to an external location, such as all skills and chats. |
| 8 | Integration & architecture design | 🟡 | This part makes sense to me, as I understood the basics quite well, so it's easy to walk through all the steps. However, if I see it again in further weekly sessions, it will probably come to my mind even more easily. It was good to go through the steps to understand how my build could work in real life. For my Feedback-to-Task Assistant, the project manager copies the feedback email into the project folder, where an agent processes it into an Excel file that is updated with each new piece of feedback. The PM reviews the output and corrects it in the chat before anything is passed on to the team.|
