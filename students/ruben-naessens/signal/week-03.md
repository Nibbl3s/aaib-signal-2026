# The Recruitment AI Pitch: Are We Buying Better Hiring or Better Marketing?

**Week 3 — The Skeptic | Ruben Naessens**

A recruitment AI vendor can make an attractive pitch: spend less time reading vacancies, identify relevant qualifications faster and make better hiring decisions. For a busy recruitment agency, those promises sound like an easy investment. But there is a difference between selling software that extracts information and promising a business outcome such as better hires. That gap is where I want to apply the Week 3 FOMO test.

My own AI build is deliberately narrower. It takes a job description and returns structured JSON separating mandatory from preferred qualifications. This is useful because vacancy texts are inconsistent: one employer writes “must have,” another hides requirements in paragraphs, and multilingual vacancies add another layer of complexity. A language model might reduce the time needed to organize that information. But an output that looks tidy is not automatically accurate.

Consider a **hypothetical** vendor claim: “Cut screening time by 60%.” The percentage is meaningless without a baseline. Does “screening” mean reading job descriptions, reviewing CVs, contacting candidates or making final decisions? Were difficult vacancies included? Did the vendor count the time recruiters spent checking mistakes? If a recruiter saves six minutes but spends four minutes correcting missing qualifications, the value is far smaller than the headline suggests.

A second promise might be “better candidate quality.” That is even harder to evaluate. A tool that identifies keywords consistently could improve the organization of vacancy requirements, but it does not follow that it predicts performance or fairness. It could even turn an ambiguous preference into a hard requirement, unfairly excluding someone. The real test should count false mandatory requirements and missed essential qualifications, not just how convincing the JSON looks.

The non-AI alternative deserves equal attention. A standardized vacancy intake form with mandatory fields could solve part of the problem at low cost. For clear criteria such as possession of a required license, a simple rule is more predictable than a generative model. AI earns its place only where interpreting messy text adds enough value to justify errors, review and maintenance.

I would ask any vendor five uncomfortable questions: What baseline produced the claimed time saving? What are the critical-error rates on real vacancies? How do results vary across languages? What happens to applicant and vacancy data? What is the total annual cost, including human checks and integration? A vague answer to any of these changes the investment case.

My provisional verdict is **pilot**, not buy. I would test representative vacancies, write the expected answers before running the model, and compare the results with a manual workflow and a structured form. Human review would remain mandatory. I would only consider buying after measuring net time saved, error rates and the full cost of ownership.

The biggest lesson is that AI FOMO often begins when a plausible technical capability is treated as a proven business result. Extracting qualifications may be valuable. Promising better hires from that capability alone is a leap. In recruitment, the right question is not “Can we use AI?” but “What specific task does it improve, compared with what, and at what risk?”
