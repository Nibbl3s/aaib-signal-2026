After looking at AI costs in Week 1 and capability limits in Week 2, I now have a different question: how much work remains after the impressive demonstration?

Freightos markets a platform combining rate management, quoting, booking and AI. Its page promises “From Messy Email to Complete Search in 1 Click” and says “Never quote blind again.” These phrases sell speed and confidence. The purchasing decision needs evidence about both. [Source: Freightos](https://www.freightos.com/ai-for-freight-forwarding/).

The distinction matters because a search is not an approved freight quote. A buyer still needs to establish whether the shipment information, applicable rates and final commercial terms are correct.

| Claim or feature | My classification | What I would check |
|---|---|---|
| One-click conversion from an email to a search | Testable capability claim | Does it correctly extract details from our actual requests? |
| Confidence implied by the marketing slogan | Aspiration | What errors remain, and how are uncertainty and missing information handled? |
| Human approval before AI-generated quotes proceed | Testable workflow claim | What must the reviewer check, and how long does that take? |

Freightos explicitly describes human approval gates. It would therefore be unfair to criticise the page as promising fully autonomous quoting. However, keeping a person involved does not tell me the cost or effectiveness of that review. Those need measurement. [Source: Freightos](https://www.freightos.com/ai-for-freight-forwarding/).

My first FOMO question is the business problem. For a hypothetical European forwarder, I would investigate time spent preparing and checking quotes. Before testing software, I would record the current handling time and material error rate. Without that baseline, “faster” has no useful meaning.

Second, what happens if the company waits six months? I would calculate existing labour costs and investigate whether slow responses actually lose business. I would not assume that competitors using AI proves an urgent need to buy it.

Third, can the promise be verified? I would propose a controlled pilot using anonymised requests, including Russian and English text, ambiguous units, missing shipment details and unusual routes. Staff would establish expected answers before testing. The measure would be total time per approved quote, including corrections, rather than generation speed alone.

Fourth, the budget must include subscriptions, integration, training, human review and mistakes. I do not have a vendor quotation, so claiming a financial return now would be premature.

Finally, I would compare the tool with a structured request form and a validated rate calculator. If inconsistent customer information is the main bottleneck, improving collection might solve much of the problem without adding AI.

My verdict is a conditional pilot. I would propose at least a 25% reduction in total staff time per approved quote, with no observed increase in material errors on the agreed test set. That is my proposed threshold, not a measured result or a guarantee of future reliability.

The product may create real value. But the value I would buy is a cheaper, reliably checked quote—not the number of clicks in a demonstration.
