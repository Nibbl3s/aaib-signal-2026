# Don't Buy AI Consulting Out of FOMO: What PwC Belgium Actually Promises

## The Vendor

**Company:** PwC Belgium

**Source:** [PwC Belgium — Artificial Intelligence: Organisations & Business Strategy](https://www.pwc.be/en/services/consulting/technology-consulting/artificial-intelligence/organisations-business-strategy.html)

PwC Belgium presents AI as a way for businesses to improve productivity, support decision-making, and identify opportunities for using AI. Since I am interested in AI in business consulting, I wanted to examine these claims and consider whether a business should hire an AI consultant immediately or first investigate its actual needs.

## 1. Deconstructing the Claims

| **Claim from PwC's page** | **Classification** | **Reason** |
|---|---|---|
| “AI is ready right now to boost productivity and decision making.” | Aspiration | AI can support productivity and decision-making, but this statement does not prove that every business will achieve these results. |
| “54% of executives say that AI solutions have already increased productivity in their businesses.” | Fact — needs verification | This is a measurable statistic, but the original survey, sample, and methodology should be checked before relying on it. |
| “These leaders are using AI to automate processes too complex for older technologies.” | Aspiration | The statement describes what AI can help businesses do, but it does not provide a specific example or measurable result here. |
| “provide forward-looking intelligence to strengthen human decisions.” | Aspiration | This describes a potential benefit, but the page does not provide a measurable improvement in decision-making. |
| “To reduce errors and mitigate risk, PwC can help embed transparency and security into AI from the start.” | Aspiration | This describes a service PwC offers. It suggests reducing errors and risks, not eliminating them completely. |

The main difference is that a claim about a possible benefit is not the same as evidence that the benefit has already been achieved. The 54% statistic is more specific than the other claims, but it still needs verification.

## 2. Applying the FOMO Framework

### Q1. What specific problem are we trying to solve?

PwC discusses productivity, decision-making, and identifying opportunities for AI. However, a business should first identify its own problem. For example, it might have high operating costs, slow administrative processes, or difficulties analysing data.

If the business cannot identify a clear problem, it may be considering AI simply because it fears falling behind competitors.

### Q2. What happens if we do nothing?

Doing nothing is not always the wrong decision. If the company's existing processes work well, introducing AI may not be an immediate priority.

However, if competitors use AI to improve productivity or reduce costs, the company could miss opportunities by ignoring it completely. The decision should depend on the business's situation rather than pressure to follow a trend.

### Q3. Can the claims be verified?

PwC's page mentions that 54% of executives say AI solutions have already increased productivity in their businesses. Before using this statistic to make a decision, a business should check the original research, including who was surveyed and how the result was measured.

It should also ask PwC for relevant client examples, measurable outcomes, and evidence showing how similar projects performed. General promises are not enough to establish the likely results for a specific company.

### Q4. What is the total cost?

The landing page does not provide a specific price for an AI consulting project. A business would need to ask for a detailed proposal that includes consulting fees, software costs, implementation, employee training, maintenance, and any ongoing expenses.

Without this information, the business cannot properly compare the expected benefits with the total investment.

### Q5. Is there a simpler alternative?

Before hiring a large consulting firm, a business could improve its existing processes, make better use of its current data tools, or automate simpler tasks.

For a complex AI project, professional consulting may be valuable. However, the company should first determine whether it needs external expertise and whether the expected benefits justify the cost.

## 3. My Verdict: Pilot First

I would not recommend committing to a large AI transformation based only on PwC's landing page. I would first request a specific proposal, measurable targets, relevant client examples, and a complete cost estimate.

The next step would be to run a small pilot focused on one clearly defined business problem. The company could then compare the results with its original process and decide whether a larger investment makes sense.

My main lesson is that businesses should not buy AI consulting because they are afraid of missing out. They should identify a real problem, check the evidence behind the vendor's claims, understand the full cost, and test whether the proposed solution delivers value.

**The key takeaway:** AI consulting may create business value, but a business should establish that value through evidence and testing rather than relying on promises alone.
