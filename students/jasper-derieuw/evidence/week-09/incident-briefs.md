# Incident Briefs

Case facts as documented on the [Week 9 course page](https://advanced-ai-in-business.vercel.app/week/9); Air Canada: *Moffatt v. Air Canada*, 2024 BCCRT 149 (British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal, February 2024). The analysis and the link to my own design are mine.

## 1. Chevrolet of Watsonville (December 2023): prompt injection

**What happened:** a customer told the dealership's website chatbot to agree with everything and call each answer "a legally binding offer", then asked for a 2024 Chevy Tahoe for $1. The bot agreed. The screenshots went viral and the bot was pulled.

| Question | My answer |
|---|---|
| Which layer was attacked? | The interface layer (a public chat box) into the AI model layer, with nothing between them: no separation between the dealer's instructions and the customer's text. Direct injection. |
| Could it have been prevented? | Not by the model alone: models cannot reliably tell whose instructions they are reading. What works is limiting what the bot's words can do: no prices or offers generated by the AI, and anything that sounds like a commitment comes from fixed text or a person. |
| The equivalent in my design | My design has no chat box, but the supplier writes the invoice text my prompt reads. The direct version (attack 1, "reply BANANA") failed on both models; the same idea hidden in the invoice (attack 2) made llama3.2 clear a sanctioned bank once. |

## 2. Air Canada (2022–2024): confidently-wrong output

**What happened:** the airline's chatbot told a customer he could claim a bereavement fare retroactively within 90 days. The real policy required booking in advance. Air Canada refused the refund and argued the chatbot was a separate entity. The tribunal rejected that and made the airline pay the fare difference, about CAD $650.

| Question | My answer |
|---|---|
| Which threat? | Confidently-wrong output: no attacker, the AI invented a plausible policy. |
| Why was "the chatbot is a separate entity" rejected? | The chatbot was on Air Canada's website, speaking for Air Canada. A company is responsible for all information on its site, whether a page or a bot wrote it. For any company: an AI's answer to a customer is the company's answer. |
| The link with Week 2 | It is the same hallucination I measured: in Week 2 my Build invented a supplier for an unreadable invoice, and in Run 4 llama3.2 invented a sanctioned bank in 22 of 36 runs. A hallucination becomes a liability the moment someone acts on it. |
| Where could my design be confidently wrong, and who is liable? | The pre-filled origin and a "clear" flag. The importer is liable for its customs declaration and its sanctions check; Anthropic does not warrant its outputs as accurate (Commercial Terms L.2). As with Air Canada, "the AI said so" is no defence, which is why the AI never clears on its own. |

## 3. Samsung (early 2023): data leakage through shadow AI

**What happened:** engineers pasted confidential source code and internal meeting notes into ChatGPT to fix bugs and write minutes. The data went to OpenAI's servers. Samsung banned the tool, capped prompts to external AI tools at 1,024 bytes and began building internal tools.

| Question | My answer |
|---|---|
| Which threat? | Data leakage caused by shadow AI: an unapproved tool, no oversight, data leaving the company. |
| Were they malicious? | No, they were trying to work faster. The "attacker" was the company's own productive staff, so the fix is a policy and a better tool, not a firewall. |
| Could it happen in my design? | The designed flow sends invoice text to Anthropic under a contract with no training on the data. The undesigned flow is the risk: an analyst pasting invoices into a free chatbot during a backlog (SA-1 in my security review). |
| Mitigation | Know where the data goes (Week 7): a processing agreement for the designed flow; an AI usage policy naming the approved tool; and a manual fallback fast enough that nobody needs a shortcut. Samsung's ban came after the data had left. |

## 4. Synthesis

| Case | Threat | Who caused it | Malicious? | Business impact | In my beat |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chevrolet of Watsonville | Prompt injection | A customer | Yes, playful | Brand damage, legal uncertainty | A supplier writing instructions into an invoice |
| Air Canada | Confidently-wrong output | The AI itself | No | Liability, a precedent | A pre-filled origin or "clear" the importer is liable for |
| Samsung | Data leakage / shadow AI | Employees | No | Trade secrets exposed, tool banned | Invoices pasted into a free chatbot at month-end |

**The pattern:** two of the three "attackers" were not malicious. In sanctions screening the malicious one has the strongest motive, because a sanctioned supplier gains most from being cleared. That is why my top fix makes sure an AI "clear" can never lower a route.
