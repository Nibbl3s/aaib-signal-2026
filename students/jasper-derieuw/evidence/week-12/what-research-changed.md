# What My Primary Research Changed

## The recommendation I would have made without it

Before I measured anything (Week 1), the desk answer was easy: tokens cost about €166 a year, a vendor promises "95% of reviews automated", so **buy an AI screening product**. After my first own runs (Week 4) it became: **rules decide, the AI only adds warnings, worth €82,033 a year from year two with a payback of 3.4 months**, still resting on a check time I had assumed, a fine I had looked up and vendor claims nobody had confirmed. Without primary research, that is where I would have stopped.

## The recommendation I make now

**A three-month pilot on one product family with known suppliers. The AI proposes, rules route and a person decides every invoice. No vendor is signed without three terms in writing, and outside counsel confirms the risk class before go-live. The pilot stops if an honest check takes more than three minutes or if it misses a single sanctioned match.** One-off cost €28,007–€28,607; saving €7,298–€25,280 a year from year two; payback 13–47 months (est., formulas in the pitch notes and Weeks 8–10).

## What it did and did not change

It did not change the direction: a cautious yes, through a pilot. It changed almost every number under it. The saving fell from about €82,000 to €7,298–€25,280 a year, partly because I costed my own design honestly (Week 8) and partly because every practitioner added a control that costs time or money. The payback went from 3.4 months to one to four years.

That is a smaller case and a stronger one. In October my figures came from brochures and my own assumptions. Now the accuracy comes from 60 measured calls, the check time from a practitioner, the design from two practitioners who tried to break it, and the vendor terms from a silence I can date. The one load-bearing number that is still not measured, the time of an honest check, is exactly what the pilot measures first, and the fablab review test on 18 November is my first attempt.
