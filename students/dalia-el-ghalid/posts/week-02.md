When exam week gets close, it's super tempting to use ChatGPT or Claude as a personal tutor. You paste in a study question, type out a quick answer, and ask it to tell you if you're right. It feels fast, convenient, and productive. But when you actually test this against a basic "No AI" framework, you realize it's one of the riskiest ways to study.

First, there's the money side. Whether you're paying $20/month for a student subscription or running API tokens to process long study guides and practice answers, you're paying for a tool to evaluate you. But paying for AI feedback only makes sense if the output is actually reliable. If you spend money on an AI that gives you bad feedback, you're literally paying to get misled.

The core issue isn't that AI can't write feedback. The problem is that language models are built to sound encouraging and fluent—not to check your answer against your professor's exact slide deck or exam rubric. 

When you ask an AI to grade an open-ended answer, two main problems happen:
1. False confidence (hallucination): The AI tells you your answer is great simply because it sounds well-written, even if you missed the actual core concept.
2. Context drift: It grades you based on general internet definitions instead of the specific vocabulary your professor wants to see on the test.

If you run this through the 5-question decision framework:
* Cost of error vs. speed: The cost of being wrong here is high. If an AI gives you a fake green light on a concept you didn't actually understand, you're going to lose marks on exam day.
* Verification: To know if the AI was even right about your grade, you still have to open your slides and double-check anyway.

If you have to open your slides to check the AI's grading, it didn't save you time or money—it just added extra steps and cost.

The simpler tool that actually works better? Basic active recall with free flashcards (like Anki) or self-grading side-by-side with your slide deck. Write your answer down from memory, then compare it directly with the official definition. It takes 30 seconds, costs zero euros, doesn't hallucinate, and actually builds real retention. 

AI is great for reformatting messy lecture notes into clean tables, but when you're testing whether you genuinely know the material for an exam, stick to the real answer key.

The question I ask is: What do you want to learn next? At what point does feeding an AI your exact course slides make automated grading reliable enough to actually trust for exam prep?
