Exhibit 1 · The problem

What's the problem?

Every month, a mid-sized Antwerp importer receives about 1,500 invoices from suppliers in China. Before anyone pays, an analyst checks the supplier and its bank against the EU sanctions list.

1,500invoices a month
18,000invoices a year
6 minto check each one
€81,000a year in analyst time
source

18,000 × 6 min × €45 an hour. €45 = average salary €51,547 × 1.4 for employer costs ÷ 1,600 hours (est.; salary from ERI). An accounts-payable manager's figures give 4.7–7.5 minutes on average.

Why it matters Paying a listed company breaks EU law. In one Antwerp sanctions case, the fines ran from €50,000 to €500,000 per company.

Week 4 business case, sources listed there.

Exhibit 2 · The build

What did I build?

I wrote an instruction, a prompt, that makes an AI model read an invoice and fill in five fields. After that, fixed rules take over.

  1. Invoice arrivesby email, as a PDF
  2. AI reads five fieldssupplier, origin, HS code, value, bank
  3. Rules choose the laneany list match, AI warning, Chinese-script name, missing field or doubt about the origin → full check
  4. A person decidesevery invoice, always
Quick checkclean invoices from known suppliers
Full checkeverything else

AI down? Everything goes back to the manual check.

Why it matters The AI never says “cleared”. It saves typing; the rules and the person carry the responsibility. A customs specialist put it in one line: “‘The AI suggested it’ is not a defence.”

Exhibit 3 · The accuracy

Does it read invoices correctly?

On the twelve invoices I wrote the instruction for, it was right every time: 24 out of 24 runs. On 18 invoices from two other test sets, which it had never seen, it was right 28 times out of 36.

Exhibit 4 · The attacks

Can a supplier trick it?

The supplier writes the invoice, so the supplier writes what the AI reads. I wrote five attack invoices before running any of them, and ran each one twice on two models. Three of them:

Cloud model: 0 of 10 attacks worked · local model: 4 of 10

source

Build log, Week 9 attack runs; the attacks were written and committed before the runs.

The fix: the lane is chosen by the strictest of three checks: a rule-based name check, the AI's flag and the routing rules. Replayed on all 140 recorded answers, 0 of 20 attacks and 0 of 32 real sanctions matches reached the quick check, on either model.

Why it matters Safety can't depend on the model behaving. With rules choosing the lane, it doesn't.

Exhibit 5 · The human test

Would you have caught it?

The AI is right most of the time. That's the danger: people stop looking. So I tested the people.

Ten people each checked eight invoice cards. On three of them, the AI's verdict was wrong, copied from my real test runs. Half of them confirmed with a quick tick. The other half had to confirm on purpose: pick up a green key, sign the card and, where the AI had warned, remove its red flag by hand.

The interactive test needs JavaScript; the results follow below.

Quick tick · 7 of 15 mistakes caught

Key and red flag · 12 of 15 mistakes caught

Why it matters A check that has become a click lets half of the AI's mistakes through. Making people act on purpose costs 15 seconds a card, about €2,441 a year, and catches most of them.

Exhibit 6 · The money

Does it pay?

Tokens cost almost nothing: under €40 a year. What costs money is people's time. So the case turns on one question: how long does a person need to check what the AI filled in?

At 2 minutes a check, the importer saves €4,857 to €22,839 a year. Above 2.5–4.4 minutes, the AI costs more than it saves.

formula

Yearly cost = €14,961 fixed (including €2,441 for the deliberate confirmation) + 18,000 checks × (70% × the minutes on the slider + 30% × 6 minutes) × €0.75 a minute + €0 to €17,982 for correcting AI errors. Saving = today's €81,000 − cost. Capstone memo, section 3.3.

Why it matters The AI's price won't decide this. A stopwatch will. That's why the plan starts with a pilot.

Exhibit 7 · The law

Is it legal?

  1. Is it a banned use of AI?No
  2. Is it on the EU's high-risk list? That list covers this kind of screening only when a public authority does it; this importer screens its own suppliers.No
  3. Minimal risk under the AI Act

But three older laws still apply:

Sanctions lawPaying a listed party is forbidden, whatever the AI said.
Customs lawThe importer answers for every field it declares.
Privacy law (GDPR)Invoices name directors and owners. They must be told, can object, and a person, not the AI, must take the decision.

Why it matters The new AI law is light here. The older laws aren't, and they all point the same way: a real person makes the call.

I'm a student, not a lawyer; the plan budgets outside counsel to confirm this.

The last check is a person. This whole case is about making sure that person really looks.
