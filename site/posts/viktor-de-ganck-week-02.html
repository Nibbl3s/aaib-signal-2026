I Don't Need AI to Tell Me the Fed Raised Rates

Viktor De Ganck · 2026-10-07

When the Federal Reserve raised rates on 16 September, my first instinct was to ask an AI what it meant for the dollar, for stocks and for crypto. That instinct is exactly what this week's "no AI" framework is built to question. For one part of my beat, central bank decisions, the honest answer is that AI is the wrong tool.

What I wanted AI to do

My plan was simple: let AI follow the Fed and the ECB, tell me what they decided, and explain what it means for currencies, equities and commodities. It sounds like the perfect job for an AI research desk.

The framework stops at question one

The first question is: do you already know the answer? For the decision itself, yes. An economic calendar like ForexFactory lists every rate decision with its exact time, the forecast, the previous value and the actual result the moment it is published. It also rates each event by expected market impact, which filters out the news that barely moves markets. It is free, it is instant, and it cannot invent anything. The framework says: if you know the answer, use that. There is nothing left for AI to do.

What happens if you use AI anyway

I tested this in my own Build, a prompt that classifies financial news items. One input was an X post by a commentator: the market-implied odds of a rate hike had risen from 0% to 6.2% for April and to 15% for June. By my rules, that is an opinion. The model labelled it as an official rate decision in one run and as macro data in the next, both times as a fact. Same input, two different answers, and both wrong in the most dangerous direction: speculation turned into news.

A calendar cannot make that mistake. It only shows decisions that were actually taken.

Where AI still adds something

The decision is a number; its meaning is not. What a 25 basis point hike does to the euro, to bank stocks or to Bitcoin depends on what the market already expected, and that is where AI could help: summarising the statement, comparing it to the previous one, listing the usual market reactions. But question two of the framework applies here: is the cost of being wrong higher than the cost of being slow? With my own money at stake, yes. AI can draft the interpretation, but I have to check it against the calendar and the official statement before I act on it.

The trade-off

On accuracy, the calendar is exact, while my AI tool was fully right on 9 of 15 test items. On speed, both are instant. On cost, the calendar is free; the AI is almost free but needs checking time. On trust, the calendar shows its source, while the AI shows confidence, not certainty. For the decision itself, the simpler tool wins on every count.

The question

If AI can turn a forecast into a "fact" with total confidence, would you trust its explanation of a rate decision with your own money, or only after checking it yourself?
