Can an AI Agent Find Your Freight Forwarder? A GEO Audit of DREWES Group

Beat: AI and Communication Automation in European Freight Forwarding
Target Company: DREWES Group (Bremen/Benelux)

The Hook

When a supply chain manager needs a reliable partner to move heavy project cargo through Bremen or Antwerp, they are no longer just scrolling through ten pages of Google links. They are asking AI assistants to synthesize a shortlist. To find out who actually makes that cut, I ran a Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) audit on a mid-sized European forwarder. The results are a stark wake-up call for traditional logistics marketing.

The Company

I chose to audit the DREWES Group, a Bremen-based freight forwarder with operations extending into Belgium. Founded in 1925, they specialize in ocean and air freight, heavy-lift project cargo, and complex supply chain solutions. They are exactly the kind of agile, mid-sized specialist that AI should be recommending to enterprise procurement teams. But as I discovered, having a century of operational expertise does not automatically make you visible to modern algorithms.

The Audit

I evaluated DREWES against the six-criterion GEO framework. They scored just 5 out of 18, putting them firmly in the AI-Invisible category. Here is how that breaks down:

Criterion Score Audit Findings
Structured Data 0/3 A look at their page source reveals zero Schema.org or JSON-LD markup. Without this, AI web crawlers have to guess what services DREWES provides rather than reading a neatly categorized digital manifest.
Content Parseability 1/3 Technical capabilities are buried in long marketing paragraphs. There are no clear comparison tables or direct Q&A formats that an AI can easily extract and quote.
Third-Party Mentions 1/3 Outside of standard corporate registries, DREWES is missing from the digital logistics publications, supply chain tech reviews, and industry case studies that train modern large language models.
AI Citation Presence 0/3 When prompted to recommend mid-sized forwarders in the Bremen and Benelux region, Perplexity and ChatGPT never cited DREWES. The models defaulted entirely to global giants like Kuehne+Nagel or better-indexed local competitors.
Authority Signals 1/3 The site highlights their century-long history, but it lacks the published operational metrics, expert bylines, or technical whitepapers that signal authority to an AI.
Brand Direct-Search 2/3 Exact brand searches do bring up their website first, but they lack a robust Google Knowledge Panel or Wikipedia presence to solidify their status as a recognized entity.

The Verdict

If an automated procurement tool were asked to recommend regional logistics partners today, DREWES would not make the list. The issue is not their operational quality; it is their digital citability. Because they exist almost exclusively on their own website without structured data or multi-source validation, generative models essentially treat them as unverified. An AI needs third-party consensus to confidently make a recommendation, and right now, DREWES only has one source validating their expertise: themselves.

The Insight

This audit highlights a massive blind spot in European freight forwarding. For decades, companies have relied on direct relationships and basic SEO. But as shippers begin using AI to research and summarize market options, forwarders without structured data and dense third-party mentions will simply be left out of the conversation. They risk being disintermediated before a customer even drafts a request for quotation.

The Question

As enterprise procurement shifts toward automated AI discovery, how long can heritage logistics providers rely on their offline reputation before digital invisibility starts costing them real revenue?
