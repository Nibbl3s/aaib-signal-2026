Build, Buy, or Connect? Avoiding Vendor Lock-In in Hiring

In recruitment, getting trapped by a software vendor rarely happens overnight. More often, it creeps in quietly when an AI provider updates its system and breaks your workflow, or raises prices once your team depends on it. Without checking the architecture, HR teams risk losing control over hiring quality, candidate data, and budget.

Consider a Swedish recruitment agency reviewing job ads before publication. Every month, recruiters check hundreds of drafts for biased phrasing, missing legal details, and unclear requirements. Instead of reviewing everything manually, the team connects a commercial cloud language model via API to check drafts against their internal HR rules. At €0.0013 per ad, the model evaluates each text and flags errors or formatting slips before ads go live.

Choosing this setup comes down to five questions: build an AI, buy a finished HR tool, or connect an existing model to your own instructions. First, is the AI model our competitive advantage? No. Agencies win by placing great candidates, not owning AI, whereas our HR rules actually matter. Second, does an off-the-shelf tool work? No, because generic grammar checkers miss Swedish labor norms. Third, do we need multiple AI models? No, one strong text model handles the review. Fourth, will our needs change in twelve months? Yes, hiring rules evolve constantly, requiring quick edits to our instructions. Fifth, do we have engineers to maintain an in-house model? No, and testing a small laptop model got zero out of ten ads right versus three out of ten for the cloud model. Connecting a cloud model to our own instructions is therefore the right path.

Even a lightweight connection scores 8 out of 15 (moderate risk), across five dimensions. Data portability (1/3) stays low because ads and outputs remain in local files without API storage, though saving bulk history takes extra logging. Migration cost (1/3) is also low since no database sits on vendor servers. However, API stability (2/3), pricing control (2/3), and feature dependency (2/3) carry higher risk. Costs scale with volume without promised advance notice for price hikes, tricky HR rules demand a strong model, and saving instructions inside a vendor's web tool ties us to their platform. API stability is the highest risk because the provider does not guarantee identical answers after updates, meaning a silent change can break our layout.

If the agency had to switch to a rival cloud provider tomorrow, moving would take roughly one workday of around eight hours to adjust the instructions and test Swedish hiring edge cases. For a few days, formatting and consistency would likely dip, forcing recruiters to double-check more drafts manually until the wording is tuned. Over the next ninety days, three simple habits prevent lock-in without over-engineering. First, store all HR instructions in plain local files rather than a vendor's dashboard. Second, use a simple, replaceable connection layer, known as a thin wrapper, that requests answers in a standard format like JSON so switching providers only means changing one connection key. Third, keep ten real job ads with known flaws to test whether a new or updated model still catches real mistakes before live use.

Many HR software vendors pitch AI screening as an all-in-one black box, locking buyers into rigid contracts and opaque scoring. Yet this case shows that the real business value sits in a recruitment team's own HR rules, not the AI model. Keeping instructions, formats, and test ads portable lets agencies treat AI providers as replaceable utilities rather than permanent landlords.
