Can AI Really Replace a Customer Service Employee?

Lima · 2026-10-08

Last week, I explored the financial potential of replacing traditional customer service with AI. The calculations suggested that AI could significantly reduce operational costs for a solo entrepreneur. However, one important question remained: how reliable is AI when dealing with actual customer enquiries?

This week, I decided to test that question myself.

Building an AI Customer Service Classifier

I created a fictional online clothing store called SoloShop, operated by a single entrepreneur. The goal was to develop a simple AI tool that classifies incoming customer messages into three categories:

AUTOMATE: Routine enquiries that can potentially be handled automatically.

HUMAN INTERVENTION: More complicated or sensitive cases requiring personal attention.

INSUFFICIENT INFORMATION: Messages that are too unclear to classify.

Using Google AI Studio and Gemini, I developed a prompt containing the company's customer service policies and classification rules.

Initially, I tested the classifier with fictional customer enquiries. The result was impressive: 100% accuracy. But I quickly realised that these messages were relatively straightforward and had been written specifically for my test.

Would the results be the same with real customer messages?

Testing with Real Customer Enquiries

I collected ten publicly available customer questions and complaints, including messages in English, Dutch and Spanish. Each message was tested twice to examine both accuracy and consistency.

The results were different from my first experiment.

Accuracy: 70% — 7 out of 10 messages matched my expected classifications.

Consistency: 100% — Gemini returned the same classification in both tests for every message.

Three disagreements — The model differed from my expected answer for three inputs.

One customer reported that an order had not arrived and that they could not access their account because they had purchased as a guest. I expected the AI to classify this as AUTOMATE, since automated troubleshooting could be the first step. Gemini instead selected HUMAN INTERVENTION.

Another message involved a content creator proposing a collaboration. I initially expected INSUFFICIENT INFORMATION, but Gemini selected HUMAN INTERVENTION.

Looking more closely, I realised that Gemini's answer was arguably more appropriate. The request was perfectly understandable, but it required a business decision rather than standard customer support.

What Did I Learn?

My biggest discovery was that an AI classification can disagree with my expected answer without necessarily being wrong.

Some of my classification rules were incomplete. I had defined how to handle shipping, returns and complaints, but not collaboration requests or messages unrelated to customer service.

I also learned that consistency and accuracy are different. Gemini produced identical responses in both runs, even when they disagreed with my expectations.

For a solo entrepreneur, this matters. An AI tool that consistently escalates too many enquiries could create unnecessary work, while one that fails to escalate serious complaints could damage customer relationships.

My Conclusion

AI has potential to support customer service in a one-person business, particularly when interpreting messages written in different languages and styles. However, my experiment showed that clear policies and well-designed tests are just as important as the AI model itself.

My next step is to improve the classification rules and retest the same messages to see whether accuracy increases without sacrificing consistency.

The question is no longer simply whether AI can automate customer service, but how much human judgement is still needed to make that automation reliable.
